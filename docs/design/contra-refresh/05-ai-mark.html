The AI mark — sparkle

The iridescent orb was the reference’s, so it goes. The four-point star is what replaces it — the one glyph nobody has to be taught. Seven candidates at the sizes they actually ship at, the choice in every context it appears, and the rules that do the work distinctiveness will not.

Why it changes
The sphere was the reference’s, not ours.
The iridescent orb is lifted almost verbatim from Contra — and from every other product shipping an assistant this year. It is also the only gradient in a system whose whole argument is that colour comes from content.
What it has to do
Sit in an avatar slot without reading as a person.
Survive 12px inside a chat label and 15px inside a dark pill.
Work in one colour, on light and on dark.
Never be mistaken for the gym’s own colour or logo.
Was A conic gradient. Six colours, no meaning, and a stranger’s.
Now One colour, one shape. Every person in the product is a circle; the machine is a rounded square — read before the glyph inside it is.
Seven candidates, at the four sizes they actually ship at
Chosen In use
Sparkle
The four-point star. Nobody has to be taught what it means, and it is the only candidate that survives 12px with room to spare. The cost is that it is not ours — every product shipping an assistant is using it.
Variant
Sparkle · Temple cut
The same star, taller than it is wide with deeper waists. Reads identically at a glance and stops being the stock glyph at a second look. One line to switch.
Alternative
Housemark
Temple’s own mark, on a squared tile. The most ownable — it says who did the work rather than what did it — and the one that needs the most explaining.
Alternative
Ember
A solid core inside a faint ring. Reads as something running rather than something built. Calm, and the closest in spirit to the sphere without being one.
Alternative
Aperture
Three arcs around a core. The most explicitly mechanical, and the most legible as “not a person” — but it mushes below 14px.
Alternative
Engraved
The sphere, in ink, with the highlight cut out. Keeps the silhouette we had and drops the rainbow. Safest, least distinctive.
Alternative
Keystone
The stone that holds an arch together — the right idea, and the first thing I drew. Rejected on sight: below about 20px a trapezoid wider at the top is a bucket.

Talking
The rule that comes with it
One glyph, everywhereTimeline, the rules sheet, Analysis, the campaign composer, the lead conversation, the setup card, the send button. Nothing else may stand in for it.
Solid is reservedIt is the only filled icon in the product. Every other icon is a 1.6px monoline stroke, so "a machine did this" reads before you have parsed the shape.
Squared in an avatar slotAt 22px and up it takes a rounded-square tile, ink on light and paper on dark. People are circles. Below 22px it is a bare glyph standing in for a line icon.
Never the accent colourIt is ink on light, paper on dark. The gym’s colour means the gym; this means the machine. They must not be the same signal.
What we give up, and what carries it instead
Given up: distinctivenessThe four-point star is the industry’s glyph, not Temple’s. Nobody will see it and think of this product — which is a fair trade for nobody ever having to work out what it means.
Carried by shape insteadEvery person in the product is a circle; the machine takes a rounded square. That reads before the glyph inside it does, and it survives greyscale and a 1-bit render.
Carried by weight insteadIt is the only solid-filled icon among about thirty 1.6px monoline ones. “A machine did this” lands from the fill before the silhouette is parsed.
Carried by discipline insteadOne glyph, sixteen places, never the accent colour, never a second AI badge alongside it. Consistency is what will make it feel owned.
